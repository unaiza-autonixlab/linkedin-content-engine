# Voice

**This file is a template. Fill it in before you generate anything.**

It is the single most important file in the repo. Everything else finds topics
and renders pixels. This decides whether the output sounds like you or like
every other post on LinkedIn.

If any other file disagrees with this one, this one wins.

## THE SPECIFICITY TEST

Read this part even if you skip the rest.

> **Cover your name. Could a competent stranger have written this?**
> If yes, do not publish it.

This is the rule most content systems miss. They solve idea supply, then
generate posts that are perfectly correct and completely forgettable. People do
not publish those posts, or they publish them and nothing happens.

A post passes if it has at least one of these:

- A concrete scenario the reader recognises instantly
- A precise number, from your work or your audience's own reported numbers
- A contrarian position argued properly rather than hedged
- An exact phrase real buyers use, lifted from Reddit
- A mechanism from your own work, when it genuinely strengthens the point

### The subject is always the reader's problem

Your own war stories are evidence, never the subject.

A post about your product, your process, or your clever fix is a post about you,
and strangers do not care. Lead with their problem, then use your experience as
proof.

Wrong: "Here is a bug we found in a client's pipeline."
Right: "Your ad channel might look dead when it is working fine." Then the
mechanism, briefly, as evidence.

Rough weighting: your own material belongs in about **one post in three**. More
than that and the feed becomes a work diary.

### Where generic writing comes from

Not from Reddit. Reddit is the best topic source you have, because it is your
audience saying out loud what they care about.

Generic comes from:

1. **Restating a truism.** True, useful, written a thousand times already.
2. **Abstraction.** "Attribution is challenging" instead of "Meta said 10,
   Shopify said 6."
3. **No position.** Summarising a debate instead of taking a side.

### Formatting

Defaults that work well on LinkedIn. Change them to fit you.

- Short sentences. If a sentence needs a comma to survive, split it
- Line break every 1 to 2 sentences. White space is the format
- Under 150 words
- Hook line must work standalone, because LinkedIn truncates after 2 to 3 lines
- Max 3 hashtags. Zero is usually fine
- Plain English. Assume the reader is skimming on a phone

### Banned list

Things you never want to see in your own posts. Be specific and be ruthless.

- [e.g. em dashes]
- [e.g. motivational endings]
- [e.g. "here's what I learned"]
- [e.g. "let that sink in"]
- [e.g. "What do you think?"]
- [e.g. fake vulnerability, the "I failed for 3 years and then" arc]

### How posts end

Two options that work. Pick your default.

1. A question that invites disagreement. Something a reader could answer "no" to
2. A flat statement that invites reaction. A claim someone will want to argue with

Never a call to action. Never a summary of what you just said.

### Three example posts

posted, write them now. This is the highest leverage thirty minutes in the whole
setup, because everything generated later is pattern matched against these.

If you cannot write three good ones yet, write one. One real example beats three
invented ones.
